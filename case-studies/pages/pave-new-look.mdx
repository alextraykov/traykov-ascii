## Summary

In late September Pave by Quickbase had two problems at its front door. The home page put a gradient behind the prompt that looked like every other AI tool, and light mode was so flat that panels disappeared into the page. Product leadership wanted light mode to become the default before a marketing push.

I designed both at once, in code, and handed engineering decisions it could build in days. The blueprint home merged within a day of engineering's first commit; light mode went from handoff to merged in under a week. Both went live for every Pave user in one release on 15 October, and the next morning the marketing site moved onto the same sheet. For the first time since launch, the website and the product looked like one thing.

## The blueprint

The gradient said "AI" without saying what Pave makes. So the brief became: the background shows what Pave builds (tables, forms, workflows, connectors, portals) drawn the way a draftsperson draws them, and always quieter than the prompt box. I explored thirty coded directions before product leadership picked one.

A generative background has one job it must never fail: don't clip a drawing or open a cell under the prompt box. Early on, no sampled cell passed. I built an audit that scores every open cell on five checks and wrote layout rules until every cell passed, on desktop and phone, inside the frame budget at four times CPU slowdown.

## Light mode

The finding was small and a little embarrassing: every surface was the same tone, so nothing looked higher than anything else, and the green sidebar was the loudest thing on screen. It didn't need new colours. It needed an order.

```case-swatches title: Three layers, from colours Pave already shipped
Background | #f1f3f5 | #111715 | The frame, sidebar and chat
Raised | #ffffff | #161d1b | Preview pane, page tiles, tables, the composer
Floating | #fcfcfd | #272a29 | Menus, popovers and dialogs
```

Three rules carry it: the composer is the only white object in the chat, there's one green button per page, and in dark mode a higher layer is lighter.

Engineering merged it within a week.

## One release

Running two redesigns at once had one real cost: the blueprint file and the light mode file drew the sidebar differently, and engineering had to ask which was right. I dropped the frosted variant and folded the blueprint's card into the raised layer, so one system now covers the front door and the builder.

The day after the merge I measured the build against the spec on every main page, not only the ones with a design to compare against. It scored 8.0 out of 10, and the gaps were mostly pages I hadn't drawn. Product took over that list.

## What I'd keep

1. Design in code and hand over decisions. That's why each merge took days, not weeks.
2. Structure before palette. Solving light mode with existing colours made it cheap to approve and easy to carry into dark mode.
3. Audit every page, not only the ones with a design to compare against.

Still open: the wider error family and the phone layout are designed and waiting, and until someone measures it, I can say people liked it, not what it changed for the people who use Pave.
