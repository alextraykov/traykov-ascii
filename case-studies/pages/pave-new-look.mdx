## Summary

In late September, the home page of Pave by Quickbase had a gradient blob behind the prompt box. Our CPO didn't like it. Fair: it looked like every other AI tool, and it said "AI" without saying anything about what Pave actually makes.

I replaced it with an animated drafting sheet. Its lines breathe slowly, and its cells open to draw blueprints of the things Pave builds: tables, forms, workflows, connectors and portals. Each drawing builds itself in the order a draftsperson would draw it, then takes itself apart.

Seventeen days later the same sheet sat behind the home page, sign-in and the account and access pages for every Pave user. A day after that, the Quickbase web team shipped it on the marketing site. What people see before they sign up now matches what they get after.

- **Role** Design engineer: direction, motion system, prototypes, layout audits, specs and handoff
- **Team** I designed and specified it. Austin Colcord engineered the product version. The Quickbase web team built the marketing-site version from my package.

- **Status** Live for every Pave user since 15 Oct 2026. Live on [quickbase.com/pave](https://www.quickbase.com/pave) since 16 Oct 2026. Several surfaces below are concepts and are labelled that way.

## The problem

The gradient had no referent. It sat in the same soft-glow territory as the tools we compete with, and nothing in it told you Pave builds business software.


My first counterpoint was the wrong kind of opposite. If the blob was too soft, surely something technical would read as serious. The CPO rejected the technical reference straight away as too technical for our audience, and they were right. Pave's builder is someone who runs operations at a desk, not a developer. A terminal look would have swapped one costume for another.

There was a second problem underneath. Sign-in and error pages didn't look like the product at all. When I audited them I found four unrelated visual styles across 48 surfaces, including framework defaults that showed a bare "Not Found". You could sign out of a polished builder and land somewhere that looked like a different company.

## The brief: construction, not atmosphere

I rewrote the brief around one idea: the background should show what Pave builds, not set a mood.

```case-quote
attribution: From my design handoff
The grid is a spreadsheet, the foundation that grows into apps, workflows and their components, so it never moves for its own sake.
```

Four principles held everything else in place:

1. Every mark must be something a non-technical person can name. If you can't say what a shape is, cut it.
2. The centre is a desk. Idle motion is slow enough to ignore, and anything you cause answers within about 100ms.
3. Drawn, not popped. Nothing scales from zero, bounces or spins.
4. The prompt box is the focus. The grid stays quieter than its edge and never sits under it.

## Exploring widely, then narrowing fast

I started with two research decks: 12 app-builder competitors, glyph and ASCII trends, and six live prototypes. Then three brand-background directions with three variations each, and six deliberate opposites, so the CPO could see the whole space and not one corner.

Confession: the "whole space" part wasn't my instinct. I was already fine-tuning animation curves when a designer on the team told me to show two or three drastically different directions first. Annoyingly correct. It got a decision in one review.

The grid prototype began with 24 variants. Over 23 numbered rounds in two days I cut it to 10, then 6. In the first review the CPO picked the blueprint and asked for less: the grid was over-rotating on busy, so no drift, no pointer parallax, and motion tied to what the user is doing.

## How the sheet works

Vertical lines never move. Free horizontal lines breathe up to 52px about every 12 seconds. Up to four cells are open at a time, with a new one about every eight seconds.

Each cell draws a blueprint in the order a draftsperson works: lattice, centrelines, outline, rules, lettering, hatching, anchors, dimensions and the title block. It takes about 7.7 seconds to draw and 3.7 to take apart.

Hover over empty grid and a ruler measures the cell. Click bare sheet and a leader line is drawn. Focus the prompt box and the nearest lines slide onto its edges to frame it.

## Making a generative animation reliable

A generative background has one job it must never fail: don't clip a drawing, squash a column or open a cell under the prompt box. For a while it did all three, and "it looks a bit off sometimes" was the most precise thing I could say about it.

So I built an audit that samples every open cell and scores five checks: clipped, title overlap, centred, filled and stable. Then I wrote layout rules until every cell passed. At the start, none passed and 60% of samples had a squashed column. Eight rules later, every cell passed, on desktop and on phones.

- **Before** 1,677 cells sampled, 0% pass all five checks, 60% squashed columns
- **Rules 1–3** 1,593 sampled, 74% pass, 0% squashed
- **Rules 4–6** 1,378 sampled, 89% pass, 0% squashed
- **Rule 7, three seeds** 3,466 sampled, 97.2% pass, 0% squashed
- **Tighter column gap** 1,290 sampled, 99.2% pass, 0% squashed
- **Final tune, rule 8** 1,083 sampled, 100% pass, 0% squashed
- **Phones** 56 sampled, 100% pass, 0% squashed

Performance stayed inside budget too. At four times CPU slowdown, p95 frame time stayed between 9 and 13ms against a 16ms budget. That's a local test, not field data.

## Light, dark and reduced motion

Dark mode gets the same proportional quieting as light, not the same contrast. I tried equal contrast first. It turned the green muddy and made the grey grid louder than the drawing, which is exactly backwards.

With reduced motion on, the sheet shows one still frame of finished drawings and nothing moves. The canvas is decorative and hidden from screen readers.

## From prompt to builder

I went through about seven versions of the moment you press send. The colour flood looked dramatic in a recording, which is why I kept iterating on it long after it should have died. It read as tacky, then aggressive, then choppy, and the frame strips agreed: at four times CPU slowdown it took 2.3 seconds and dropped 10 frames, and its edge read as a stepped staircase even at a perfect frame rate.

The chosen version, "disperse", takes 0.6 seconds with no dropped frames. The page leaves least important first, the grid breaks into dashes, and the finished builder appears in one step. I recorded at 40ms intervals to find what was wrong with each version. This transition is still a concept; it is not in the product.

## Sign-in, account and error pages

I carried the sheet to every page outside the app, so no one lands on a page that looks like a different product. Each page is one card on a quieter sheet, with no navigation, built from the design system's components. Fonts are self-hosted, so words appear at first paint, about 50ms instead of up to 2.4 seconds.

Every page has its own blueprint illustration drawn as a real drafting symbol. A missing page is a dashed sheet at the end of a leader that reached nothing. Access denied is a door symbol with a swing it does not make. A suspended account is a striped site barrier.

Sign-in has one choreography for every outcome. On success the card lifts and one drawing opens. On failure an inline message draws in. Never a shake, never red on the sheet: a wrong password is not a broken thing.

I packaged the sheet for the marketing team, with a walkthrough video, the exact motion values and 11 checks for the build. The web team rebuilt the quickbase.com/pave hero from it, including the sheet and the typed suggestions in the prompt box. The "Sign in" link now takes you to the same sheet inside the product.

It also shipped my mistake. The chips under the website's prompt box came from placeholders in my package, and some of them name things Pave doesn't build as standalone apps. Nobody else's fault: when another team builds from your files, anything unfinished needs a label they can't miss, and mine didn't have one. As of 16 Oct the fix was still being decided.

## Handoff

Engineering got one source of truth: a spec set covering every value, running prototypes, an audit harness, target screenshots, films and a one-file fix. Engineering applied the handoff as delivered and ported the prototype engine line for line.

It wasn't frictionless. Engineering raised six scope questions in three days, and they were good ones: was this the whole homepage or just the animation, and why did the sidebar appear when we'd discussed dropping it the day before? My handoff hadn't said clearly enough what was in scope. I answered all six together and pointed to one page instead of a long thread. Engineering kept every sidebar link and dropped the glass sidebar, which looked cool and wasn't used anywhere else.

## What I learned

1. Show broad directions before polishing one. A designer on the team pushed me to do this early, and it got a decision in one review.
2. The next step has to stand out more than the animation behind it. That became a rule: the grid is always quieter than the prompt box.
3. Mark placeholders loudly. Placeholder chips from my marketing package went live on the website. When another team builds from your files, anything unfinished needs a label they can't miss.
4. Audits beat opinions. Scoring every cell turned "it looks a bit off sometimes" into rules engineering could build to.

What's still open: register, verify, reset, the wider error family and the published-app pages are designed and still in my queue. The send transition, typed suggestions in the product and the phone layout are concepts. And until someone runs the numbers, the honest summary is that people inside Quickbase liked it a lot, and I don't yet know what it changed for the people who use Pave.
