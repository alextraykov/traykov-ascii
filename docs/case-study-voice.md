# Case-study voice

Extracted from `case-studies/pages/designing-pave.mdx`, the study that sounds most like Alexander. Use it for every case study. `AGENTS.md` still owns the hard rules (no invented facts, credit, plain words); this file is about how the sentences sound.

## How the sentences work

- **Short and declarative.** Subject, verb, object. "Plan Mode had become common across GenAI platforms, so we needed one too." Most sentences are under 20 words.
- **Then one dry line.** After a run of plain explanation, a short sentence lands the point with a bit of wit, and is never forced:
  - "You can judge how well that worked in the navigation rework. It really did not."
  - "For a design prototype, it was robust as hell."
  - "It was flexible in the way a box of loose screws is flexible."
  - "'We will sort auth out later' is how demos become archaeological sites."
- **A rule worth keeping, on its own line.** When a section teaches something, it ends on one sentence that could be pinned to a wall: "The prototypes made one rule clear: show the effect before asking somebody to commit." "'Go back' sounds harmless until the interface shows how much work 'back' contains."
- **Admissions that cost a little.** Alexander is part of the problem: "I started to confuse the size of the repository with how easy it was for the team to use." "My favorite unshipped artifact was the Command Center. I would cut it."
- **Product mechanics as evidence.** Real states, real component names in code style (`ClarifyCard`, `Approve & Run`), real counts ("84 end-to-end checks", "Button used in 76 files").
- **Credit by name, warmly.** "Matthew Schweers was the engineering brain behind all of this (shoutout)." "Nick discovered 76 files." Say who did what, and what Alexander did not do.
- **Honest limits, stated once and plainly.** "This is a before-and-after read, not a causal result." "I can show the reasoning and the result, but I will not invent the final speech." One clear sentence, not a paragraph of hedging.
- **Asides with dates.** "2026 update: enterprise customers still ask for them often."

## What it never does

- No marketing verbs: unlock, drive, empower, elevate, seamless, delightful.
- No "X, not Y" slogans as headings, and no punchy two-part headings.
- No hedging chains ("it's worth noting that…"), no throat-clearing openers, no closing moral.
- No disclaimers repeated across sections, and no "That's reception, not…" filler.
- Never "I shipped": engineering shipped it from the handoff.

## Headings

Plain sentences that name the move: "Plan before spending credits", "Give email notifications an actual editor", "Rebuild navigation around the product Pave had become". Sentence case, no colons.
